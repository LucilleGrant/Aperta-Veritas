# Boundary Testing

## Definition

**Boundary Testing identifies where observation, representation, testing, comparison, or revision stops, applies a represented test across that limit, and observes what changes.**

The boundary, test, response, distinctions, measurements, support relations, comparison basis, attributed functions, evaluator, conditions, and residuals remain separate records.

Boundary Testing does not assume that a boundary is either protective or restrictive, necessary or unnecessary, accurate or inaccurate.

Its function is to expose relations that can be examined through Recursive Truth Exposure.

## Why boundaries require testing

Every inquiry operates through limits:

- categories;
- methods;
- permissions;
- scopes;
- resource budgets;
- ethical constraints;
- institutional rules;
- identity;
- language;
- attention;
- available observations;
- available distinctions;
- available measurements;
- available support relations;
- available alternatives;
- retrieval;
- compute;
- memory;
- time.

Boundaries can preserve, exclude, compress, allocate, transform, or conceal represented relations.

The same boundary can perform several functions under different conditions.

A boundary is not epistemically closed merely because it stops an operation.

Operational stopping and epistemic closure are distinct.

Closure occurs when a representation is insulated from relevant distinction or when conditions for revision are suppressed.

A finite boundary can therefore remain open when its basis, effects, limitations, and conditions for reconsideration remain represented.

## Procedure

### 1. Map the boundary

Identify where observation, questioning, testing, comparison, revision, representation, action, or permissible speech stops.

Record:

- the explicit boundary;
- detectable implicit boundaries;
- who or what enforces it;
- the stated justification;
- the represented conditions under which it applies;
- the consequences of crossing it;
- the observations, claims, relations, actions, distinctions, or alternatives it places outside the active process;
- whether conditions for revision are represented;
- whether conditions for reopening are represented.

Do not infer closure merely from the existence of a limit.

### 2. Identify the evaluator

Record the evaluator under which the boundary is being examined.

Identify:

- criteria;
- distinctions;
- operationalizations where applicable;
- measurements where applicable;
- support relations;
- comparison basis;
- values;
- conditions;
- exclusions;
- dependencies;
- desired outcomes;
- relevant resource constraints.

The evaluator is part of the inquiry.

A boundary can produce different evaluations under different criteria, distinctions, or comparison bases without those evaluations being silently collapsed into a single judgment.

### 3. Apply a test

Introduce one or more represented tests such as:

- a counterexample;
- an inversion of the represented perspective;
- an excluded implication;
- an excluded evaluator;
- an inactive branch;
- an unrealized alternative;
- a change of distinction;
- a change of scale;
- a change of context;
- a new observation;
- a conflicting measurement;
- a conflicting support relation;
- a change in comparison basis;
- a divergence between a declared account and observed selection behavior;
- a case where the same rule changes retained states for a different actor or condition.

The test is an instrument, not a verdict.

Crossing a boundary does not establish that the boundary was false, harmful, unnecessary, or closed.

Preserving a boundary does not establish that it was true, beneficial, necessary, or open.

### 4. Observe the response

Record what changes and what remains invariant.

Questions can include:

- Does the represented conclusion revise?
- Does represented support change?
- Does the boundary move?
- Does its scope change?
- Does the justification change?
- Does the evaluator change?
- Does a relevant distinction change?
- Does an operationalization change?
- Does the comparison set change?
- Does the comparison basis change?
- Does the system process the new observation?
- Does it exclude the observation?
- Does it redirect evaluation toward the source or evaluator?
- Which consequences become newly represented?
- Which representation remains unavailable?
- Which measurements become possible or impossible?
- Which support relations become available or unavailable?
- Which branches become active or inactive?
- Are reopening conditions preserved?

A response is recorded before its function is assigned.

### 5. Attribute possible functions

Represent hypotheses about what the boundary may be doing.

Possible functions include:

- protection from an event represented as harm;
- representation of a material constraint;
- allocation of scarce resources;
- compression serving a represented operation;
- preservation of identity;
- preservation of authority;
- preservation of system continuity;
- concealment of dependency;
- maintenance of an adaptation produced under earlier conditions;
- prevention of an observation or measurement;
- restriction of available distinctions;
- restriction of available support relations;
- restriction of available comparison sets or comparison bases;
- reduction of resource cost;
- confinement represented as protection.

Multiple functions can coexist.

A function attribution is a claim, not an intrinsic property automatically discovered by the test.

Record the observations, data, measurements where applicable, tests, logical relations, provenance, explanatory relations, consequences, other represented support, evaluator, and conditions relevant to each attribution.

A measurement does not become support merely by existing.

A support relation does not become a measurement merely because it participates in evaluation.

Where no represented basis supports comparison among competing function hypotheses, preserve them without manufacturing a ranking.

### 6. Record the selection operation

Record:

- state before selection;
- state after selection;
- evaluator;
- criteria;
- distinctions;
- operationalizations where applicable;
- measurements and other represented results;
- support relations;
- active states;
- inactive states;
- excluded states;
- comparison set;
- comparison basis;
- dependencies;
- resource conditions;
- declared account;
- observed transition;
- residuals;
- stopping conditions;
- reopening conditions.

Selection does not establish improvement, accuracy, necessity, or truth by itself.

### 7. Compare boundary hypotheses

Compare represented hypotheses only under an explicit represented comparison basis.

A comparison basis can include:

- the alternatives being compared;
- relevant distinctions;
- criteria;
- predicted effects;
- observed effects;
- contradictions;
- logical relations;
- provenance;
- explanatory relations;
- reproducibility;
- scope;
- robustness under changed conditions;
- resource effects;
- excluded observations;
- reversibility;
- dependencies;
- support relations;
- measurements where applicable;
- effects on represented support;
- effects on available comparison sets;
- evaluator;
- conditions.

Comparison does not require every relevant relation to be converted into a common measurement.

Where comparison depends on measurement, preserve the relevant distinctions, operationalization, measurements, methods, and conditions.

A shared measurement does not by itself establish that two hypotheses are defensibly comparable.

Absence of a shared measurement does not by itself establish that comparison is impossible.

When no represented comparison basis supports a ranking, preserve the competing hypotheses without ranking them.

When relevant relations remain incommensurable under the represented comparison basis, preserve the incommensurability.

A comparative result remains conditioned by the represented support, comparison basis, comparison set, evaluator, methods, and conditions under which it was produced.

### 8. Revise relationally

Possible transition branches include:

- preserve the boundary;
- restrict its scope;
- expand its scope;
- relocate it;
- make it conditional;
- change its evaluator;
- change its criteria;
- change a relevant distinction;
- change an operationalization;
- change its comparison basis;
- open it to specified observations;
- replace it;
- remove it;
- retain competing boundaries as explicit branches;
- stop inquiry while preserving reopening conditions.

Each transition records:

- provenance;
- evaluator;
- criteria;
- distinctions;
- operationalizations where applicable;
- measurements and other represented results;
- represented support;
- comparison basis;
- exclusions;
- resource use;
- affected branches;
- residuals;
- genealogy;
- stopping conditions;
- reopening conditions.

Previous boundary states remain genealogically represented rather than being overwritten.

## Boundary Testing and closure

Boundary Testing distinguishes a boundary from closure.

A boundary can stop an operation while remaining open to revision.

Closure indicators include:

- relevant observations are excluded without preserving the exclusion;
- relevant distinctions are prevented from entering examination without preserving that exclusion;
- revision conditions are removed;
- reopening conditions are suppressed;
- a boundary is exempted from examination by category alone;
- confidence, authority, consensus, value, or policy status substitutes for represented support;
- support is reduced to a measurement by category alone;
- comparison is forced without representing its basis;
- conflicting branches are erased rather than retained;
- the evaluator or criteria are concealed;
- the boundary's attributed function is treated as intrinsic rather than tested;
- stopping is represented as finality.

These indicators identify relations for further inquiry. They do not by themselves establish the truth or falsity of the bounded claim.

## Semantic invariants

Boundary Testing preserves the distinctions:

```text
boundary != closure
pressure != disproof
resistance != proof
function attribution != intrinsic function
distinction != measurement
support != measurement
conclusion != support
selection != improvement
stopping != closure
```

These are current architectural invariants, not claims exempt from Recursive Truth Exposure.

## Recursive requirement

Boundary Testing applies to itself.

The tester introduces boundaries through:

- selected tests;
- chosen language;
- available observations;
- available distinctions;
- operationalizations;
- available measurements;
- represented support relations;
- relevance judgments;
- evaluator selection;
- comparison sets;
- comparison bases;
- resource allocation;
- stopping conditions.

Adversarial pressure, inversion, and transgression do not gain support merely by opposing an established limit.

Likewise, preservation, safety, authority, and stability do not gain support merely by maintaining one.

The tester's framing, desired result, values, exclusions, distinctions, methods, support relations, comparison basis, resource limits, and power remain available for examination.

The boundary test itself therefore produces another inquiry state rather than an epistemically privileged verdict.
